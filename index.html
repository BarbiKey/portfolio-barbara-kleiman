A role system for a translation platform, designed and built.

Admin Platform is the admin console of an enterprise real-time AI translation product for contact centers, used by teams running on major CCaaS platforms. Four roles, three tenant levels, and a lot of room to get permissions, and protected call data, wrong.

26PRs merged, real shipped code
4roles across the tenant model
3nested tenant levels
40+documented use cases

The problem

Supervisors, Admins, and Super Admins needed different views of the same data, scoped to tenants and organizations that could nest three levels deep. Without a clear model, every new screen risked leaking data, including PII and HIPAA-protected call content, across a boundary it shouldn't cross.

The model

I defined the role and tenant architecture and translated it into an RBAC matrix and a mock data model, then documented 40+ use cases across MVP and Phase II and 12 user flows covering dashboard, sessions, teams, and replies, with page-level specs and acceptance criteria engineering could build from directly.

The agent's screen

Agents needed to follow the live transcript of a call without losing track of call controls, so I designed a dedicated screen with real-time transcription, plus a floating widget for when they stepped away from it mid-call. It also surfaces their call history and the preset replies their supervisor has given them. The screen started as a Figma Make prototype, and I carried it into real code from there, in React, TypeScript, and shadcn/ui, so engineering had a working reference instead of a static file.

Supervisor & Super Admin

+

On the other side, Supervisors and Super Admins needed full operational control: reviewing sessions, managing preset replies, teams, languages, settings, and custom model assignment, without that control ever exposing protected call content. I designed and built an audit log viewer, a HIPAA toggle, and a retention policy selector as first-class parts of each portal, not bolted on after the fact.

From prototype to merged code

+

This isn't a standalone mockup. It's 26 of my PRs merged into the real shared codebase, reviewed and merged by the engineering team. It lives on a parallel redesign track rather than having replaced production yet, but it was built, reviewed, and shipped the way real product code is.

Live monitoring and a safety-critical confirmation loop, both designed and coded.

Call Platform is the contact-center call product of that same translation platform. Two problems inside it needed more than a Figma file to prove they worked: a supervisor's live view into call health, and a real-time safeguard against an AI interpreter mishearing a caller's address.

8+commits to the Monitoring Console
3weeks, console to live

The platform underneath

I designed the full call-control surface this case sits on: the dashboard, agent and phone-number management, call-control pages, role definitions, and the live transcript screen agents see during a call, as the foundation the Monitoring Console and agent-view work below were built into.

Monitoring Console

Supervisors had no single place to see call health as it happened. I designed and built the Monitoring Console end to end: live call monitoring, quality trends, health signals, search and filters, across 8+ commits over three weeks. I set up the project's Cloudflare deploy pipeline myself, so every push would deploy automatically.

The problem: an interpreter that has to be sure

+

In the agent view, the real-time screen of the Call Platform, an AI interpreter can mishear a caller's address or phone number mid-call. Passing that through unverified is a real risk, more so on a 911 or other time-critical line. Asking the caller to repeat it wasn't enough on its own: the fix needed a confirmation flow an agent could trigger instantly without derailing the call.

Active PII Verification

+

I designed and coded the confirmation loop myself, reusing the agent view's real production components rather than a disconnected mockup: an agent-initiated trigger (a side-panel button or a /verify address command), a live confirm/cancel flow, and two different attempt caps depending on urgency, four attempts for enterprise calls, two for time-critical ones. I scoped what wasn't ready yet just as deliberately: no fabricated confidence score, no auto-trigger until Engineering confirms the latency it would need.

Language re-detect, shipped

+

Inside the same prototype I designed and built the language re-detect flow: trigger, status pill, confirm/cancel, tab lockout for a wrong-language call. The engineering team built the production version directly from this interaction spec.

Flōra, cycle tracking that explains itself instead of prescribing.

Flōra is a self-initiated React app combining hormonal cycle tracking, strength training programming, nutrition guidance, and symptom monitoring in one place.

The decision that shaped it

Most cycle apps sell "cycle syncing" as settled fact. I chose evidence-based framing instead: the app shows what the research actually supports and leaves the prescriptive language out, even where it would have made the copy punchier.

Building it myself

Nine iterations so far, built and rebuilt as I learned what the data model needed to hold. It runs entirely on localStorage for now, a deliberate scope cut so I could validate the product idea before building cloud sync and accounts.

Cuponera, a coupon book for favors between people, not businesses.

Cuponera lets one person create a book of redeemable personal favors as a gift for someone else, a format without an obvious competitor to copy from.

Starting small on purpose

Rather than jump to native apps, I scoped the first version as a web MVP to validate the concept before committing to a store launch on Google Play and the App Store.

A visual language of its own

The first real test of the concept was a birthday gift: a vibrant, pop-art-styled coupon book, which also became the first real exploration of what the product's visual identity could be.

I'm a UX/UI and Product Designer based in Buenos Aires. From 2022 to 2026, as a contractor, I worked on enterprise real-time AI translation platforms for contact centers. My background started in project management across graphic design, 3D, and animation before I specialized in UX/UI, which is probably why I care as much about how work gets organized and handed off as about how it looks.

Most of my day is split between interface design, role and permission architecture, UX copy, and the documentation that makes a product legible to the people building it: specs, use cases, dev notes. Lately that documentation has started turning into working prototypes I build myself, a direction I'm pushing further through a current UX Engineering workshop with UX Unicorn.
